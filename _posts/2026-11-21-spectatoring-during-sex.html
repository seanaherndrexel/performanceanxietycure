The fastest way to lose an erection is to check on it. A man can be fully aroused, kissing a partner he wants, and the moment he wonders whether he is hard enough, the answer starts changing for the worse. Nothing physical happened. He simply stepped out of the experience and started grading it.

Sex therapists call this spectatoring during sex. The term goes back to the early clinical research on sexual function, where researchers noticed that anxious patients described sex as if they were watching it from across the room. They were present in body and absent in attention, running a quiet commentary on their own performance while the actual pleasure went unnoticed.

Spectatoring is the engine inside most performance anxiety. Understanding how it works makes it far easier to stop.

A second person in the room

Spectatoring feels like having two versions of yourself in bed. One is the participant, the man who is touching, kissing and enjoying himself. The other is the inspector, standing off to the side with a clipboard, checking firmness, timing, her facial expressions and whether this is going the way it should.

The inspector believes he is helping. He thinks that if he watches closely enough, he can catch a problem early and fix it. In practice, he is the problem. Every check he runs pulls attention away from sensation, and sensation is what keeps arousal going.

Men who spectate often describe the same thoughts on a loop. Is it still firm? Is she bored? How long has it been? What if it goes soft when I reach for a condom? Each question sounds reasonable on its own. Together they amount to a full-time job that leaves no room for pleasure.

What spectatoring during sex does to the body

Your nervous system runs two broad modes. One is protective, built to scan for danger and prepare you to deal with it. The other is relaxed and pleasure-oriented, and that is the mode erections depend on. Blood flow to the penis increases when the body feels safe enough to focus on enjoyment.

Spectatoring during sex tells your brain the opposite. Constant monitoring is what people do when something might go wrong, so the brain reads all that self-checking as evidence of a threat. It shifts toward the protective mode, and arousal drops. Desire is still present. It has just been overruled.

Then the loop closes. You notice the erection softening, which confirms the fear, which makes you watch more closely, which softens it further. By the next encounter, the inspector shows up before anything even starts. Normal fluctuation looks like failure to an inspector

Here is a fact most men never hear: erections naturally rise and fall during sex. A partial softening during foreplay, a pause, a change of position or a moment of distraction is ordinary. For a man who is fully present, it passes without a thought, and the erection returns as soon as attention goes back to what feels good.

For a man who is spectating, that same normal dip looks like the beginning of disaster. He sees it, reacts to it, and the reaction turns a small, harmless fluctuation into a real loss of erection. The body was doing something completely normal. The interpretation is what made it a problem.

Trying not to watch is still watching

Once men learn the word spectatoring, many of them try to stop it by force. They tell themselves to stop thinking, to focus, to get out of their heads. This rarely works, and the reason is simple. Telling yourself not to monitor is another form of monitoring. You are now watching yourself to see whether you are watching yourself.

The Performance Anxiety Cure makes this point directly: effort is the fuel for performance anxiety. The harder you work to fix it in the moment, the more you confirm to your brain that something is wrong. Breathing tricks and mental techniques can help for a night or two, but they tend to wear off because they keep the focus on the problem.

The more reliable approach is to change what the moment means, so the inspector has nothing to inspect.

Give your attention somewhere better to go

Attention has to land somewhere. If you simply try to empty your mind, it drifts straight back to the familiar worry. A better move is to give it a destination that has nothing to do with your erection.

The most useful destination is your own pleasure. Notice what her skin feels like, how warm it is, the taste of a kiss, the sound of her breathing. These are concrete sensations, and the brain can stay with them in a way it cannot stay with a vague instruction to relax.

This ties into a principle from the book called oxygen mask first. Many anxious men spend the whole encounter focused on her experience and on whether they are measuring up. Putting your own enjoyment first feels selfish, but it is the opposite. Your arousal is what she responds to, and a man who is clearly enjoying himself is far more attractive than one who is anxiously working.

Her arousal helps too. Foreplay that focuses on her gives your attention a natural place to rest, and her response tends to calm your nerves rather than add to them. You stop being the subject of the test and become a participant in something shared.

Take away the test

The inspector only exists because there is something to grade. If an erection is the pass mark, every second becomes an exam. Remove the exam and the inspector has no job.

One of the most effective ways to do this is to accept, ahead of time, that you might not get hard tonight and that this is fine. The book calls this fail on purpose. It sounds backwards, but when you stop needing a particular result, the pressure drops, and arousal often returns on its own. Plenty of men find that the night they give themselves permission to fail is the first night in months that everything works.

Saying something out loud helps as well. One short, casual sentence before things start, something like letting her know you sometimes get in your head and just want to enjoy being with her, takes the secret out of it. Spectatoring thrives on hiding. When she knows, there is nothing left to protect, and silence no longer leaves her guessing that she is the reason things went quiet.

Practical moves for the next encounter

None of these require forcing anything. They are ways of setting up the situation so spectatoring has less to work with.

  • Slow down the start. Spend longer on kissing and touch, with no goal attached. Arousal builds best when nothing is being measured.
  • Pick one sensation. When you catch yourself checking, move your attention to a single physical detail, such as the feel of her back under your hand. Do this gently, without scolding yourself.
  • Treat softening as normal. If your erection dips, keep kissing and touching. Stay close. It usually comes back when you stop watching for it.
  • Shorten the gaps. Long pauses, such as fumbling with a condom or switching positions, give the inspector time to step in. Plan these moments so they happen smoothly, and always use protection.
  • Use what turns you on. Be honest with yourself and your partner about what you enjoy. Treat it as a tool that pulls your attention into the moment, not a requirement that has to be met.
  • Talk to a doctor if needed. If erection problems happen in every situation, including alone, get a medical check before anything else. If a doctor suggests medication, follow their guidance on how to use it.

The day you stop noticing

Spectatoring rarely ends with a dramatic breakthrough. Men who get past it usually describe something quieter. They are in the middle of sex one night and realize they have not checked on themselves once. The inspector left at some point, and they never saw him go.

That is how this kind of anxiety fades. You stop feeding it, you stop giving it a test to run, and the brain gradually learns that the bedroom is safe. The habit of watching yourself loses its purpose, and pleasure takes its place again.

Here is the short version:

  • Spectatoring during sex is the habit of watching and grading yourself instead of participating.
  • Constant self-checking signals danger to your nervous system and pulls you out of the pleasure mode erections depend on.
  • Erections naturally fluctuate, and reacting to a normal dip is what turns it into a problem.
  • Forcing yourself to stop watching backfires. Redirecting attention to sensation and your own enjoyment works better.
  • Removing the pass or fail test, and saying one honest sentence beforehand, takes away the inspector's job.
