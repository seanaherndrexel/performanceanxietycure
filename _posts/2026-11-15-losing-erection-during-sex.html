Most men who keep losing their erection during sex have nothing wrong with their bodies. They start out hard, things are going well, and then somewhere between kissing and intercourse the erection fades and refuses to come back. The body that worked five minutes ago seems to have switched off, and the man is left lying there wondering what just broke.

Nothing broke. What happened is a shift in which part of the nervous system is running the show, and that shift follows rules you can learn. Losing erection during sex feels random and humiliating in the moment, but it's one of the most predictable things the male body does once you see the pattern behind it.

This article walks through why it happens mid-act, the habits that keep it going, and the changes that make it fade for good.

Check the pattern before anything else

Doctors usually start by asking where and when the problem shows up. That single question sorts most cases.

If you get firm erections on your own, wake up with them, and get hard easily during the early stages of being with someone, your hardware is working. The erection is being interrupted, not failing to form. That points toward anxiety and attention rather than blood flow.

If you have trouble getting or keeping an erection in every situation, including alone, see a doctor before you try anything in this article. Erection problems can be an early sign of heart, circulation or hormone issues, and those deserve a proper medical workup. The rest of this piece is written for the first group, which is the larger one among younger and otherwise healthy men.

Two systems, one body

Your nervous system has two broad modes. One is built for protection: it speeds the heart, tightens muscles and pulls blood toward the parts of the body you'd need to fight or run. The other is built for rest and pleasure, and erections depend on it. Blood has to flow into the penis and stay there, and that only happens while the relaxed system is in charge.

The Performance Anxiety Cure describes this as the fear system versus the pleasure system. They can't both drive at once. When your brain decides the moment is risky, even a little, the fear system takes over and the erection loses its supply.

The tricky part is that anxiety and arousal feel very similar from the inside. A racing heart, quick breathing and a buzz of nervous energy show up in both states. The label your mind puts on those sensations decides which system wins. Call it excitement and arousal builds. Call it danger and it drains away.

The observer in the room

Sex therapists have a name for the habit that flips most men into fear mode: spectatoring. Instead of feeling what's happening, you step outside yourself and watch. You check how firm you are. You track how long it's been. You study her face for signs of disappointment.

Every one of those checks sends the same message to your brain: something here needs monitoring, which means something here could go wrong. The fear system hears that and responds. Attention that should be on touch, warmth and her body goes to surveillance, and arousal can't survive that.

Where losing erection during sex usually starts

Ask men when they lose their erection and a large share give the same answer: right before or right as penetration starts. Foreplay goes fine. Then there's a pause to reposition, reach for a condom or line things up, and in those few seconds the erection fades.

That window is the danger zone because it's the moment of highest stakes. Everything up to that point felt like play. Now it feels like a test, and the brain knows it. With nothing pleasurable happening to hold your attention, the mind fills the gap with worry.

The fix is to shorten those transitions and keep physical contact going through them. Stay close, keep touching, and don't stop everything to set up the next stage. The book covers two body techniques for this window specifically, because it's where so many men get caught.

Condoms deserve their own mention. Putting one on is a common trigger because it creates a pause, a bit of fumbling and a loss of sensation all at once. The answer is not to skip protection. Practice putting condoms on alone until it's automatic, have one open and ready before things heat up, and ask your partner to help so the moment stays part of the intimacy instead of a break from it.

Softening is normal, and treating it as an emergency makes it worse

Erections rise and fall during sex. That's ordinary physiology, even for men with no anxiety at all. A change of position, a distraction or a pause can soften an erection for a moment, and it usually returns once arousal picks back up.

The problem starts when a man reads that normal softening as the beginning of a disaster. He panics, tries harder, and in doing so hands control straight to the fear system. A small dip becomes a full loss because of how he reacted to it.

When you notice yourself softening, keep going with what feels good. Kiss her, touch her, focus on her pleasure for a while. Foreplay does half the work in sex, and her arousal has a way of calming yours. Many men find their erection returns on its own once they stop staring at it.

It's also worth putting your own enjoyment first. The book calls this oxygen mask first: when you're absorbed in what feels good to you, you stop monitoring, and that makes the experience better for her too.

Effort is the fuel

When men start losing their erection during sex regularly, they almost always respond the same way. They try harder. They concentrate. They search for a breathing trick, a supplement or a new routine that will force the body to cooperate.

Each of those moves tells the brain that sex is now a problem to be solved, which keeps the fear system on alert. The central idea in The Performance Anxiety Cure is that this kind of anxiety fades when you stop feeding the fear. Effort is what keeps it alive, and when you stop supplying it, the fear loses its grip.

The same logic explains why so many popular fixes disappoint. Pills marketed online, abstinence challenges, pelvic exercises and diet changes all target a body that, for most anxious men, already works. Tricks can help for a night or two, then wear off, because they don't change what the brain believes about sex.

Take the test away

One of the most effective resets in the book runs against instinct. Before sex, accept that you might not get hard, and decide that's fine. The method is called fail on purpose, and it works because it removes the test. Once nothing depends on the erection, the brain has nothing to guard, and arousal is free to come back.

Some couples take intercourse off the table entirely for a while and enjoy everything else. With the pressure gone, many men find their erections behave normally, often better than they have in months.

A short, honest sentence before things start also helps. Something casual like "I get in my head sometimes, so if I lose it for a minute, it's no big deal" changes the whole room. Silence does the opposite. When an erection fades and nothing is said, many partners quietly assume they're the problem, and that tension feeds the next encounter.

If it happens and she reacts badly, stay close to her, reassure her that it has nothing to do with how attractive she is, and keep being intimate in other ways. Pulling away is what turns one soft moment into a lasting memory for both of you.

Putting it together

If you keep losing your erection during sex, the most useful steps are:

  • Confirm the pattern. If erections work alone and at night, your body is likely fine. If not, see a doctor first.
  • Notice when you start watching yourself, and bring your attention back to touch and sensation.
  • Treat softening as normal and keep going with foreplay instead of panicking.
  • Shorten the pause before penetration and keep physical contact through it.
  • Plan around condoms by practicing alone and keeping one ready, never by skipping them.
  • Say one honest, casual sentence to your partner before things start.
  • Stop trying so hard. Remove the test, and let arousal return on its own.

Most men don't feel the anxiety leaving. They just notice one day that they haven't thought about their erection in weeks.
