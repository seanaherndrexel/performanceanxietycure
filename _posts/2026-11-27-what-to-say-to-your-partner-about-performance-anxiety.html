Most men with performance anxiety are afraid of the wrong thing. They dread losing an erection, but the part that does the real damage is the silence afterward. A soft moment with no explanation leaves your partner to fill in the blank herself, and she almost always fills it in with something worse than the truth.

She decides you aren't attracted to her. Or that you're thinking about someone else. Or that she did something wrong. Meanwhile you're lying there convinced she thinks less of you, and neither of you says a word. The next time you're together, there are two anxious people in the bed instead of one.

Knowing what to say to your partner about performance anxiety breaks that pattern faster than almost any technique. The words don't need to be clever or long. They need to be short, casual and said at the right moment. Below are the lines that work, when to use them, and the mistakes that turn a simple sentence into a heavy conversation.

Her version of the story

Sex therapists see the same misunderstanding over and over. The man assumes his partner is judging his body. The partner is usually judging herself. When an erection fades and nobody explains it, the most common conclusion a woman reaches is that she isn't desirable enough. That belief makes her pull back, quiet down or start trying too hard, and every one of those reactions adds pressure to you.

This is why talking about it is part of the fix and not a side issue. Your words change what she believes, and what she believes changes how she acts. When she knows you're nervous and not uninterested, she relaxes. When she relaxes, the room feels safer to your nervous system, and arousal has a chance to come back on its own.

The Performance Anxiety Cure treats this as one of the core moves in the whole method, under the idea that you should say it out loud instead of hiding it. Hiding takes effort, and effort is exactly what keeps performance anxiety alive.

One sentence before anything starts

The best time to say something is before the clothes come off. A single casual sentence, said early, takes the surprise out of whatever happens later. You aren't confessing a problem. You're giving her a small piece of information so she knows how to read you.

Keep the tone light, the way you'd mention that you're a little tired or that you're bad at directions. A few versions that work:

"Heads up, I sometimes take a little while to get going with someone new. I'm really into you, so if it takes a minute, don't read anything into it."
"I get a bit nervous at first because I like you. If my body's slow to catch up, that's all it is."
"No pressure tonight. I'm happy just being here with you, whatever happens."

Notice what each line does. It reassures her that the attraction is real. It frames any hiccup as normal and temporary. It lowers the stakes for both of you. And it means that if your erection fades, nothing needs to be explained in the moment, because it has already been explained.

Many men find that once they say the sentence, the problem shows up less often or not at all. Saying it removes the secret you were trying to protect, and the fear loses its grip once there is nothing left to hide.

If it happens in the moment

Sometimes the erection fades before you've said anything, or even after you have. The worst thing you can do is go silent, roll away or apologize over and over. All three look like rejection from her side of the bed.

Stay physically close. Keep touching her. Then say something short and warm that keeps the moment going instead of ending it:

"Don't worry, that's just nerves. You feel amazing. Come here."
"My head got ahead of me for a second. I don't want to stop, let's slow down."

Then keep going with whatever feels good. Kissing, touching and foreplay don't require an erection, and they often bring it back once the pressure lifts. The book calls this part of an emergency toolkit for the moment things go sideways: stay close, reassure her, and keep the intimacy moving. The message she receives is that this is a small bump and you're still fully into her.

The conversation after

If things didn't go the way you wanted, a brief check-in afterward does a lot of repair work. Keep it short, and keep the focus on her feeling good and wanted rather than on your performance.

"Sorry if I seemed in my head earlier. It has nothing to do with you. I'm really attracted to you, and I get nervous when I care about someone."

That's enough. You don't need to deliver a history of every time it happened or promise that it won't happen again. Long explanations turn a passing moment into a topic, and topics tend to come with you into the next encounter.

If she asks questions, answer honestly and briefly. Most partners respond with relief, because the real explanation is far easier to hear than the one they had invented.

What to say in a longer relationship

With a girlfriend or wife, the situation is a little different. She has probably noticed a pattern, and the silence may have built up for months. Here the right move is one calm conversation outside the bedroom, at a time when neither of you is tired or in the middle of something.

Lead with the attraction and the cause, then tell her what helps:

"I want to tell you something, because I think you might be blaming yourself and you shouldn't. When things don't work in bed, it's anxiety. I'm very attracted to you. What helps me most is when we take the pressure off and just enjoy each other without worrying about where it goes."

That last part matters. Telling her what helps gives her a role that doesn't involve fixing you. She doesn't need to perform differently or try harder. She only needs to know that slowing down, laughing it off and staying close all make it easier.

It also opens the door to something the book encourages: honesty about what turns you on. When you can tell her what you enjoy, you spend more of your attention on pleasure and less on checking your body, which is the habit sex therapists call spectatoring.

Lines that backfire

Some things men say with good intentions make the anxiety worse. Avoid these:

  • Repeated apologies. One "sorry" is fine. Five of them tell her something is seriously wrong and keep both of you focused on the problem.
  • Blaming something else. Saying you drank too much or you're exhausted, when that isn't true, buys one night and sets you up for the same moment next time.
  • Promises about next time. "It won't happen again" creates a test, and tests are what performance anxiety feeds on.
  • Turning it into a medical lecture. She doesn't need a briefing on blood flow. She needs to know you want her and that you're a little nervous.
  • Making it about her performance. Never suggest she should do something differently to fix it. Even a gentle version of that lands as blame.

The common thread is that every one of these adds weight. Good lines remove weight. If a sentence makes the moment feel heavier, choose a lighter one.

Talking to yourself counts too

The conversation with your partner works best alongside a quieter one in your own head. If you walk into the bedroom thinking "I have to get this right," the words you say to her will sound rehearsed and tense. If you walk in thinking "I'm here to enjoy myself, and if my body is slow tonight, that's fine," the same words come out relaxed.

The book has a name for this shift. The Fail On Purpose reset means fully accepting that you might not get hard tonight and being okay with it. Once there's no test, there's nothing to fail, and arousal tends to return on its own. Pair that with the idea of putting your own enjoyment first, which the method calls oxygen mask first, and you stop treating sex as a job you might be fired from.

When your own head is calmer, the sentence you say to her stops being a cover story and becomes a simple, true statement. Partners can hear the difference.

One practical note: if erection problems happen in every situation, including when you're alone, talk to a doctor before assuming it's anxiety. Erectile trouble can sometimes signal a physical health issue that deserves a proper checkup.

The short version

  • Silence is what hurts. Without an explanation, she will assume she's the problem.
  • Say one casual sentence before anything starts. Lead with attraction and keep it light.
  • If it happens mid-moment, stay close, reassure her in a few words and keep going.
  • Afterward, a short check-in is enough. Skip the long explanations and the promises.
  • In a longer relationship, have one calm talk outside the bedroom and tell her what helps.
  • Pair your words with a calmer mindset: remove the test and put your own enjoyment first.
